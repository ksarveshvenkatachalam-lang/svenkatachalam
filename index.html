Interview coordination for global technical hiring

Playbooks and working notes on running interview processes across EMEA, AMER and APAC for teams that hire infrastructure engineers. These sit alongside my CV rather than repeating it.

Working hours Overlaps with London Outside working hours

There is no hour when London, New York and Singapore are all at work.A panel spanning all three regions has to be split across sessions or ask someone to start early or finish late. The playbooks below are about making that trade-off deliberately and fairly.

How I approach coordination

Four working rules I apply regardless of the ATS or the team.

Candidates are never the fallback

When time zones force an awkward slot, it goes to an interviewer who has agreed to it, not to the candidate by default.

Spread the inconvenient slots

Early starts and late finishes rotate across the interviewer pool, so goodwill is not used up by the same two people.

Every change has an owner and a clock

A reschedule is only finished when the candidate, every interviewer and the ATS all show the same time.

Privacy starts in the calendar

Invite titles, attendee lists and attachments are where candidate data leaks most easily, so they get the same care as the ATS.

Playbooks

Self-initiated working material. Examples use illustrative roles, people and figures, not any company's real hiring data.

Multi-region scheduling

Planning a loop when the regions never overlap

A Singapore-based candidate for an APAC solutions role, with interviewers in London and New York. Two of three regions can meet during working hours; the third cannot.

The rule I start from
Put as many interviews as possible in real overlap windows first, then solve the remaining gap with the least disruptive option.
Options for the gap, in order
Swap in an equally qualified interviewer from an overlapping region, move the session to an early start for the interviewer who has agreed to it, or split the loop across two days.
What I avoid
Asking the candidate to take more than one out-of-hours slot, or leaving more than two working days between the first and last interview.

Disruption handling

When an interviewer drops out

Late changes are routine in global hiring. What matters is that the candidate hears about it quickly and the replacement is someone who can assess the same things.

What I keep ready
A backup interviewer list for each role family, agreed with hiring managers, showing who can cover which interview and in which time zone.
How I would measure it
Share of changes resolved without moving the candidate, and time from the dropout to the candidate being told.

Reporting

A weekly snapshot that leads to action

A weekly report is only useful if every number on it has a definition, a threshold and a person who acts when it goes red.

Definitions I would agree first
Time to schedule runs from the stage move in the ATS to a confirmed invite. A scorecard is late 24 hours after the interview ends. A candidate is stale after seven days in one stage with no activity.
What the snapshot is not
A league table of recruiters. It shows where the process is slowing, by region and stage.

Notes

Short pieces on the parts of coordination that rarely get written down.

The candidate data hiding in your calendarWhy invite titles deserve the same care as the ATS.

An invite titled with a candidate's full name and the role they are leaving for can be visible to anyone who can see the interviewer's calendar. Attached CVs travel further still.

I use a candidate reference and role code in invite titles, keep interview events private, link to the ATS rather than attaching documents, and remove external attendees once a process closes.

Time to schedule is a candidate experience measureSpeed is the first thing candidates notice.

Candidates rarely see the quality of an interview kit, but they always notice how long it takes to hear back and whether the times offered make sense for where they live.

In competitive technical hiring, a quick, well-organised process tells a candidate how the company works before they have met anyone on the team.

Coordinating for people who build infrastructureSpeaking enough of the language to help.

I don't need to assess a network engineer, but I do need to know that a fabric design interview needs a whiteboard tool that works, and that the two people qualified to run it are often the same two people on call.

That understanding turns scheduling from a calendar exercise into protecting the time of a scarce team.
